Free sample: Debris hut

Why build it

For warmth and ease of construction the debris hut is one of the best. When shelter is essential to survival, build this one.

Frame

Make a tripod from two short stakes and a long ridgepole, or rest one end of the ridgepole on a sturdy base or tree at about waist height. Lean large sticks along both sides of the ridgepole as ribbing, wide enough for your body and steep enough to shed water.

  • Ridgepole running the length of the shelter
  • Ribbing wide enough for your body, steep enough to shed water

Cover and insulate

Lay finer sticks and brush crosswise on the ribbing so the insulation cannot fall through. Pile light, dry, soft material (grass, pine needles, leaves) over it, as thick as you can: the manual says the thicker the better. Add a layer about 30 cm (1 foot) deep on the floor inside.

  • Crosswise sticks first, then insulation
  • Thicker is warmer
  • About 30 cm (1 foot) of insulation under you

Close it and hold it down

Pile loose insulation at the entrance that you can drag in behind you, or build a door. Finish by laying branches or shingling on top so a storm cannot blow the debris away.

Stay alive: Keep your body working

A shelter only helps if you can stay warm, cool, fed with fire and water, and well. These guides cover the body's needs, from cold and heat injuries to fire and safe water.

  • Dress to stay warm and dryCover your head, wear loose layers, avoid sweating and keep your clothes clean and dry.
  • Sleep warmInsulate yourself from the ground, keep the shelter small and ventilated, and keep your sleeping bag dry.
  • HypothermiaRecognize the signs of the body losing heat and rewarm carefully while getting emergency help.
  • Frostbite, wet feet and snow glareSpot frostnip, frostbite, trench foot, snow blindness and sunburn in the cold, and what to do and not do.
  • Heat cramps, exhaustion and strokeRecognize heat injuries early and cool the person down. Heat stroke is a life-threatening emergency.
  • Hot weather and desert rulesUse shade and rest in the day, sip water often, and protect yourself from sand, cold nights and desert pests.
  • Tropical rain, insects and skinHandle heat, heavy rain, biting insects, small wounds and skin problems in warm, wet places.
  • Fire: site and fuelChoose a dry, sheltered, cleared spot and gather tinder, kindling and fuel before you strike a light.
  • Fire: lay it and light itLay tinder, kindling and fuel so the fire catches and burns, then light it from upwind.
  • Keep the fire going and keep it safeKeep fuel dry, bank the coals, and avoid burns, carbon monoxide and melting snow when a fire is near your shelter.
  • Drink enoughKnow your water needs, spot dehydration early and avoid drinks that make it worse.
  • Find waterCollect rain and dew, melt snow and ice safely, and make simple stills when no other water is nearby.
  • Make water safe to drinkClear dirty water, then purify it. Boiling is the safest method the manual gives.
  • Staying healthy in campKeep clean, look after your feet and teeth, rest on a schedule and keep waste away from your water.

At home: When disaster strikes where you live

Most emergencies happen near home. These guides cover what to do in a power cut, flood, storm, earthquake or fire, using public-domain guidance from Ready.gov (FEMA).

  • Power outageKeep food safe, stay warm or cool, and avoid carbon monoxide when the power goes out.
  • FloodGet to safety, stay out of flood water, and clean up carefully afterwards.
  • TornadoGet to the safest part of a sturdy building and protect your head and neck.
  • EarthquakeDrop, cover and hold on, then check for hazards and expect aftershocks.
  • HurricaneKnow your evacuation zone, shelter from wind and water, and be careful during cleanup.
  • WildfireLeave early when told, limit smoke exposure indoors, and return only when it is safe.
  • Winter weather at homeKeep warm, avoid carbon monoxide, and know the signs of frostbite and hypothermia.
  • Extreme heat at homeStay cool, drink fluids and recognize heat illness early.
  • Thunderstorm and lightningWhen thunder roars, go indoors, and stay away from plugged-in devices and running water.
  • Landslide and debris flowRecognize the warning signs, move uphill, and stay away from slide areas.
  • TsunamiMove to high ground as far inland as possible at the first natural sign or warning.
  • Volcano and ashEvacuate early if told to, and shelter from ash with sealed doors, windows and vents.
  • AvalancheMost avalanche safety happens before you go: know the risk, carry gear and act fast.
  • Emergency kit and plan basicsBuild a kit for several days on your own and agree a plan with your household.
